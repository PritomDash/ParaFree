From Zero to 36 Users a Day: Growing an Indie Tool Without Spending on Ads

By Pritom Dash · Published July 16, 2026

I want to talk about a number that probably sounds small: thirty-six.

That's how many people used ParaFree on its highest-traffic day so far. Thirty-six people in twenty-four hours. If you run a big website, that's a rounding error. But for me — a student building a tool on my own, having never paid a cent for advertising — that number was a genuine milestone, and I think the story of how I got there is more useful than another "how to get a million visitors" article written by someone who's never actually started from zero.

I'm Pritom Dash. I'm an international student in Australia, studying IT and cyber security, and ParaFree is a free paraphrasing tool I built and still run by myself. Here's the honest version of how a tool with no marketing budget grows.

The uncomfortable early truth

For the first stretch, the traffic graph was mostly flat and mostly zero. I'd built something that worked, and almost nobody was using it.

This is the part that breaks a lot of people, and it nearly broke my motivation too. You pour weeks into building something, you finally get it working properly, and then... nothing happens. No flood of users. No moment where the internet notices. Just you, refreshing your analytics, watching a number that barely moves.

I had to sit with an uncomfortable realisation during this time: building the product and getting people to use it are two entirely separate jobs, and I had only done the first one. I'd assumed that if the tool was good, people would find it. That's not how it works. Good products with no distribution stay invisible.

What actually started working

The traffic didn't grow because of one clever trick. It grew slowly, from a few sources compounding on top of each other.

The biggest surprise was AI recommendations. At some point I noticed a chunk of my visitors were arriving because an AI assistant had recommended ParaFree when someone asked it for a free paraphrasing tool. I hadn't paid for that. It happened because the tool genuinely fit what people were asking for, and the AI, when it searched the web, found it and suggested it. That taught me something: in 2026, being discoverable by AI assistants is its own channel, and it rewards tools that honestly do what they claim.

Search played a part too, slowly. As more of my pages got indexed, a trickle of people started finding ParaFree through ordinary web searches. Not a flood — a trickle. But a growing one.

And here's the pattern I noticed once I looked closely: the visitors split into two clear groups. Some arrived, glanced, and left within seconds — usually people who wanted something ParaFree doesn't do. But others stayed for real. On that thirty-six-user day, someone in Nigeria used the tool for over thirteen minutes. People in the Philippines, Kenya, and India stayed for several minutes each, clearly doing actual work. Those engaged users are the ones that matter. A tool's real audience isn't everyone who lands on it; it's the people who stay because it solved their problem.

Why "small" is not the same as "failing"

I want to push back on something, mostly for anyone else in the early, discouraging phase.

Thirty-six users a day is small, but it is not failure. Every one of those people is a real human, somewhere in the world, who found a free tool that helped them. The tool reached India, Nigeria, the Philippines, Kenya, Canada, Australia — genuinely global reach, from a student's laptop, with no ad spend.

And crucially, it's growing on its own. The fact that traffic climbs without me paying for it tells me the core idea is sound. People want a paraphrasing tool that's free, has no word limit, and keeps their formatting. When something grows organically, even slowly, it's a signal that you've built something people actually want — which is the hardest thing to know for sure and the most important.

Small-but-growing beats big-but-bought, at least this early. Bought traffic disappears the moment you stop paying. Organic traffic is a sign of real demand.

The honest gap I'm still working on

I'll be straight about my own weakness here: I'm better at building than at promoting. Left to my own instincts, I'll always choose to fix another bug or add another feature over doing the uncomfortable work of telling people my tool exists.

That instinct is a trap. At thirty-six users a day, the thing holding ParaFree back is not a missing feature. The tool already does its job well. What's holding it back is that not enough people know it's there. More features won't fix that. Only distribution will — the sharing, the posting, the actively getting it in front of people.

I know this intellectually. I'm still training myself to act on it. If you're a builder who'd rather code than promote, I suspect you know exactly what I mean, and I'd gently tell you the same thing I keep telling myself: the product is probably ready before you feel it is, and the promotion is probably more important than you want it to be.

What to take from a small number

If you're building something and staring at small numbers, here's what I'd want you to know. Slow organic growth is a good sign, not a bad one — it means real demand. The people who stay matter far more than the people who bounce. And the moment your product works well enough, your job quietly shifts from building to telling people, whether you're ready for that shift or not.

Thirty-six was a milestone for me not because it's big, but because it's real and it's mine, and it grew on its own. That's worth more than a bought number ten times its size.
