How to Paraphrase Without Plagiarising (A Student’s Method)

By Pritom Dash · Published November 4, 2026

How to paraphrase without plagiarism?

Paraphrasing without a citation is still plagiarism. A five-step method for writing a source in your own words, citing it, and using an AI tool honestly.

When I started university I thought paraphrasing meant “change enough words that it doesn’t look copied”. It doesn’t. A paraphrase that changes every word but keeps someone else’s idea without saying whose it is, is still plagiarism. Here is the method I use now — it takes a little longer and produces better essays.

What counts as plagiarism when you paraphrase?

Using another person’s idea, argument or findings without crediting them — whether you quote them, summarise them or rewrite them sentence by sentence. Changing the words does not change whose idea it is. A good paraphrase is in your own words and cited.

A five-step method

  1. Read until you understand it. Read the passage twice. If you can’t explain it to a friend, you are not ready to paraphrase it.
  2. Close the source. Write the idea from memory, in your own sentences and your own order. This is the step that stops you copying the structure.
  3. Compare. Open the source again. Did you keep the meaning? Did any phrase sneak in word for word? Put unavoidable exact phrases in quotation marks.
  4. Cite it. Add the in-text citation and the reference in the style your course uses (APA, Harvard, MLA…).
  5. Connect it to your argument. A paraphrase should do a job in your essay — follow it with what it means for your point.

Where an AI paraphraser fits

I built ParaFree’s paraphraser, so I’ll be clear about what it is for. It is not a way to turn somebody else’s text into “your” text — that is exactly the plagiarism this post is about.

When you do use it, read every sentence of the result. An AI can shift a nuance — “may cause” becoming “causes” changes a claim. Keep names, numbers and quotations exactly as they are (ParaFree tries to leave them untouched, but check), and follow your institution’s rules on AI tools; some courses require you to say you used one.

Paraphrase, quote or summarise?

  • Quote when the exact words matter — a definition, a striking phrase, a legal text.
  • Paraphrase when the idea matters but the wording doesn’t, and you need about the same level of detail.
  • Summarise when you only need the main point of a longer piece — the summarizer can help you see the key ideas of a paper before you write your own summary.

A quick self-check before you submit

For each paraphrased passage ask: is there a citation? Would the original author recognise their idea and agree it’s fairly represented? Could I explain it without looking? If all three are yes, you are fine. Run the essay through a grammar check last, so you fix the typos — not the meaning.
