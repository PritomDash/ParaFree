How to Summarise a Research Article in 15 Minutes

By Pritom Dash · Published November 4, 2026

How to summarize a research article?

To summarise a research article, read the paper in the right order, pull out the question, method, result and limits, and write a five-sentence summary — plus where an AI summarizer helps.

My first literature review took weeks because I read every paper like a novel, start to finish. A research paper is built to be read out of order. This is the routine that got me down to about fifteen minutes a paper.

In what order should you read a research paper?

  1. Title and abstract — what they asked and what they claim.
  2. Conclusion — the claim in the authors’ own final words, often with the limits.
  3. Figures and tables — the evidence, with captions.
  4. Methods — skim: who or what was studied, how many, how.
  5. Introduction and discussion — only for what you still need: the gap they fill, how they explain surprises.

Pull out five things

  • The question the paper answers.
  • The method in one line (design, sample, setting).
  • The main result, with the key number if there is one.
  • The limits the authors admit — and any you notice.
  • Why it matters for your work.

A five-sentence summary template

[Authors, year] investigated [question] using [method] with [sample]. They found [main result]. This suggests [meaning]. However, [limit]. For my [essay/project], this shows [relevance]. Write it in your own words and cite it — a summary of somebody’s paper is still their work.

A worked example: one abstract, five sentences

Here is the method on a made-up study, so you can see the shape of a good summary. Say the abstract reads: “We tested whether 10-minute retrieval quizzes after lectures improve exam scores. 240 first-year students were split into quiz and re-reading groups for one term. The quiz group scored 8 points higher on the final exam. Effects were smaller for students who already scored highly.”

  1. Question: Do short quizzes after lectures raise exam scores more than re-reading?
  2. Method: 240 first-year students, two groups, one term.
  3. Result: The quiz group scored 8 points higher on the final exam.
  4. Limits: One term, one cohort; smaller gains for high scorers.
  5. Why it matters for you: Supports using retrieval practice in your own revision plan.

Notice what is missing: no background, no list of every statistic, no copied sentences. If a summary of yours runs past five sentences, the extra ones are usually background — cut them first. When you use an AI summarizer, compare its output with these five slots; anything it leaves out (often the limits) you add yourself from the discussion section.
