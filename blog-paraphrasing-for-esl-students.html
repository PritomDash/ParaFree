Paraphrasing for ESL Students: A Practical Guide

By Pritom Dash · Published August 1, 2026

If English isn't your first language, paraphrasing can feel especially hard. You understand what you want to say, but rewording something in clear, natural English — while keeping the meaning — is a real skill that takes time to develop. As an international student myself, I understand this challenge directly. I'm Pritom Dash, originally from Bangladesh, now studying in Australia, and I've lived the experience of working and studying in a language that isn't my first.

Why paraphrasing is harder in a second language

When you write in your native language, rewording comes naturally. You have an instinctive feel for many ways to say the same thing. In a second language, that instinct is still developing. You might know one way to express an idea but struggle to find alternatives, or you might not be sure whether a reworded version sounds natural to a native speaker.

This is completely normal, and it's not a reflection of your intelligence or ability. It's just that fluency — including the flexibility to say things many different ways — takes time to build. Every second-language speaker goes through this. I certainly have.

How paraphrasing tools can genuinely help ESL learners

Used thoughtfully, a paraphrasing tool can be a real learning aid for someone building their English, not just a shortcut.

Seeing alternatives. When you have your own sentence and a tool shows you a reworded version, you're seeing another way to express the same idea in English. Over time, exposure to these alternatives builds your own sense of the language's flexibility. You start to internalize different structures and phrasings.

Checking naturalness. If you've written something and you're not sure it sounds natural, comparing it to a reworded version can help you notice awkward phrasing. It's a way to get a second perspective on your own English.

Building confidence. For many ESL students, the fear of "does this sound wrong?" holds them back. A tool that helps you refine your writing can build the confidence to write more, and writing more is how you actually improve.

How to use it as a learning tool, not a crutch

Here's the important part: use it to learn, not to avoid learning.

Write first, then compare. Always write your own version first, in your own English, before looking at a reworded one. This exercises your own skill. Then compare — what did the reworded version do differently? What can you learn from it? If you skip writing your own version and just take the tool's output, you learn nothing.

Notice the patterns. When you see a reworded sentence, don't just accept it — study it. Why is it phrased that way? What structure did it use? Noticing these patterns is how the tool teaches you, over many uses, to write better English yourself.

Keep your own voice. Your writing should still sound like you. Use reworded versions as input to your own judgment, not as a replacement for it. Pick the phrasings that feel right and that you understand, rather than blindly accepting everything. The goal is to improve your English, not to hand your writing over to a tool.

An honest note on academic honesty

Since many ESL students are also studying, I want to be clear about the same thing I'd tell any student. Use paraphrasing tools to improve your own genuine writing, not to disguise someone else's work or to dodge academic rules. Rewording your own draft to make it clearer is legitimate and helpful. Passing off someone else's work as your own is not, in any language. Use these tools honestly, and they're a genuine aid to your learning.

A word of encouragement

Learning to write well in a second language is genuinely hard, and if you're doing it while also studying difficult subjects and maybe working too — as I am — that's a real achievement, whatever your English level right now. Be patient with yourself. Every native speaker had years to develop their instinct for the language, and you're building yours while doing everything else.

Tools can help along the way, but the real progress comes from writing, comparing, noticing, and writing again. Use the tools to accelerate that process, not to skip it. The students who improve fastest are the ones who stay curious about how the language works, not the ones who just want the answer.

The bottom line

For ESL students, a paraphrasing tool can be a genuine learning aid — showing you alternatives, helping you check naturalness, and building your confidence — as long as you use it to learn rather than to avoid learning. Write your own version first, study the differences, keep your own voice, and stay honest about academic work.
